## Sizing

Bottom-up, conservative, and explicitly our estimate:

- Boomer-owned US small businesses: **2.3M** (cited).
- With SDE above ~$100k, the threshold where a sale is worth doing and an SBA buyer exists:
  ~30% → **690,000**.
- Owners within five years of exiting: ~40% → **276,000**.
- Reachable (US, has an accountant, business is not a sole trade with no transferable
  assets): ~**200,000**.
- Blended revenue per customer — $1,200 report, of which ~30% take a $15,000 programme, plus
  some transfer files — ≈ **$5,700**.

**SAM ≈ $1.1B across the wave**, roughly **$114M/year** over a decade.

As with any plan at this stage the aggregate is not the interesting number. The interesting
number is this: **$1M of annual revenue needs about 175 customers out of 200,000.** That is
0.09% of the serviceable market.

## Competitive map

### Category 1 — Sellability methodologies (adjacent, deliberately upmarket)

| Tool | Built for | Sold to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Value Builder System** | **$1M+ revenue** | **Advisors, not owners** |
| Capitaliz | Mid-market | Advisors |
| CoreValue | Mid-market | Advisors |
| BizEquity | Valuation tracking | Advisors and banks |

Value Builder is the serious incumbent and its research is good: businesses scoring 80+ on
its index sell at a **71% premium**, across 20,000+ businesses. That finding supports
Understudy's thesis rather than threatening it.

But it is explicitly built for companies at $1M+ revenue and sold through advisors. **The
sub-$1M owner cannot buy it, and it would not fit them if they could.** Two structural
reasons this is unlikely to change:

1. **The advisor channel sets the floor.** An advisor charging $5–15k for a value-building
   engagement cannot profitably serve a business with $200k of SDE. Moving downmarket would
   undercut their own channel.
2. **A score is not a work order.** Their deliverable is an index. A $200k-SDE owner does not
   need a score out of 100; they need "do these three things in this order, and here is what
   each is worth." That is a different product, not a cheaper tier of the same one.

### Category 2 — Brokers and marketplaces (the alternative, and why it fails)

BizBuySell, local brokers, M&A advisors. This is what an owner does today, and it is why
70–80% never sell. Brokers list; they do not prepare. And at this deal size the economics
simply do not work: roughly $40k of commission on a $400k sale, for nine to twelve months of
work, at a 20–30% close rate.

**Understudy is not competing with brokers — it is making their inventory sellable.** A
broker who receives a prepared business with clean books and a documented operation closes
faster and more often. That makes brokers a referral channel, not a rival. Understudy
deliberately does not broker deals, which also keeps it clear of brokerage licensing.

### Category 3 — Accountants and bookkeepers (the channel, not the competition)

They have the trust, see the books, and know who is retiring. They do not do this work —
it is advisory, not compliance, and it is outside what most of them sell. Paid 20% of what
they refer, they become the distribution system. This is the single most important strategic
choice in the plan, and it is covered in `04-go-to-market.md`.

## Why a small operator can win here

1. **The segment is structurally abandoned** by brokers on economics and by software vendors
   on channel. That is a durable gap, not a temporary one.
2. **Trust is local and personal.** A 62-year-old owner of a plumbing business buys from a
   person their accountant vouched for. That is hostile terrain for a venture-funded product
   and friendly terrain for a small, credible operator.
3. **The work resists automation** precisely because it is about getting knowledge out of
   one person's head.

The honest counterweight: these same properties cap the business. It scales by hiring and
training people, not by shipping software. See `01-business-plan.md` §7.
