# Brand

## Name

**Understudy.**

An understudy learns the part so the show can go on without the lead. That is precisely what
the product builds: the documented knowledge, the second-in-command, the transferable rights
that let a business run when its owner is not there.

Why it works:

- **It names the solution, not the problem.** "Exit planning" and "succession" both point at
  retirement, which is the thing this owner is ambivalent about. An understudy points at
  capability.
- **It is warm.** This is a business about someone's life's work. The vocabulary of the
  category — exit, succession, liquidity event, disposal — is cold and slightly funereal.
- **It carries the benefit that lands today.** An owner five years from selling does not feel
  urgency about a sale. They feel it about never taking a holiday. An understudy fixes that
  now, and the sale price is the by-product.
- **It is explicable in one sentence to a 62-year-old plumber**, which is the actual test.

**Clearance status:** "Understudy" is ordinary English and in commercial use in other
sectors. This is **not a clearance opinion** — classes 35 and 41 must be cleared by counsel,
and a modifier may be required. See `05-operations-and-legal.md`, step 2.

## Positioning statement

> For owners of established small businesses who want to step back, sell, or simply take a
> holiday, Understudy makes the business able to run without them. Unlike sellability
> software, which scores a business and is sold to advisors of much larger companies,
> Understudy prices every gap in dollars and then does the work alongside the owner — because
> a business that cannot run without you is not a business, it is a job you cannot quit.

## Voice

The category talks to these owners in one of two wrong registers: the private-equity register
(liquidity events, EBITDA multiples, value drivers) or the retirement-services register
(your golden years, legacy, peace of mind). The first is alienating, the second is
patronising.

The right register is **a competent peer who has seen a lot of businesses**, speaking plainly.

**Do**
- Use their words. Vans, crews, customers, the books, the busy season.
- Give the number, including when it disappoints. They can tell when they are being managed.
- Be specific about work. "Write down how you quote a job" beats "document your processes."
- Respect what they built. Thirty years of payroll met is a serious achievement.

**Do not**
- Use "exit," "liquidity event," "value drivers," or "legacy" in owner-facing copy.
- Imply the business is badly run. Owner dependence is how every small business starts; it
  is a stage, not a failing.
- Lead with retirement. Lead with being able to leave for two weeks.
- Promise a sale price. We estimate a range and say so every time.

**Tone:** someone sitting at the kitchen table with the accounts open, saying what they see.

## Messaging ladder

| Audience | Line |
| --- | --- |
| Owner, early | "Could your business run for two weeks without you?" |
| Owner, considering selling | "Owner-dependent businesses sell for 50–70% less. Here is what yours would fetch, and what each gap is worth." |
| Accountant | "Your clients who are retiring have no plan and 70–80% of them will not find a buyer. You see it first. We do the work; you get 20%." |
| Broker | "We make your listings financeable before they reach you." |
| Trade association | "Why most HVAC businesses in this room will close rather than sell — and the three things that change that." |

## Visual identity

The temptation in this category is either stock photography of smiling seniors on porches, or
the navy-and-gold seriousness of a wealth-management firm. Both are wrong: the first is
patronising, the second is intimidating.

- **Mark:** two overlapping brackets — one solid, one outlined, the outlined one taking the
  same shape as the solid. Understudy and lead, the same part, one learning it. Legible at
  favicon size and it means something.
- **Type:** a warm, workmanlike serif for headings — closer to a trade journal than a
  prospectus — with a plain humanist sans for body. Numbers in a monospace, always, because
  the numbers are the product and should look measured rather than asserted.
- **Colour:** a worn slate blue as the ground, a warm brick accent for the things that cost
  money, and a muted green used **only** for value recovered. Never red. An owner reading
  that their business is worth less than they hoped does not need it rendered as an alarm.
- **Imagery:** the work itself — a van, a workbench, a whiteboard with a process on it. Never
  a handshake, never a sunset, never a stock photograph of a retired couple.

## Vocabulary

One set of words, used in the product, the reports and all copy.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Transferable** | The business can change hands and keep working |
| **Owner dependence** | How much of the business only exists inside the owner |
| **Provable** | Earnings a lender and a buyer will both accept |
| **The gap** | A specific weakness, with a dollar value attached |
| **The work order** | The ranked list of gaps to close, in order |
| **Understudy** | The person or system that can run it without the owner |
| **Not listable** | It will not sell in its current state, at any price |

Never used: exit, liquidity event, value driver, legacy, golden years, disposal.
